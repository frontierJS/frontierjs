# Medical bills beat courts and tax as the best free service to build

For a small team that builds and self-hosts web apps, the best free or at-cost services to offer US individuals are medical-paperwork tools, led by a hospital charity-care application with a 240-day deadline tracker and, second, insurance-denial appeal and record-request letters. They rank first because the gap is large and documented and the legal posture is the cleanest: **only 29% of patients eligible for hospital financial assistance receive it**, **under 1% of ACA in-network denials are appealed**, and a user-signed letter drafted by software sits outside both unauthorized-practice-of-law (UPL) rules and HIPAA. Eviction and debt-collection answers have the largest need of anything researched (**about 4% of tenants have a lawyer**, and debt-collection defendants default in more than 70% of suits), but the legal gate is the tightest, so they rank third and are feasible only as one court-partnered state build. Tax filing, benefits paperwork and student-loan help follow: each has a real and time-boxed need, but each runs through channels software cannot enter (IRS e-file credentials, state portals with no sanctioned submission API, federal loan portals). The operating lesson from the precedents is consistent: services survive when someone other than the user pays for running costs (a court, a state, a hospital system, a funder buying a measurable outcome) and when the tool avoids holding sensitive data; the ones that failed carried human-heavy unit costs (Benefits Data Trust) or depended on one political patron (Direct File). Every figure below inherits the evidence caveat in the next section, and the status of *Upsolve v. James* is unverified.

## Most figures come from search summaries, not primary pages

The four research streams (legal, money and benefits, health and housing, and precedents and operating models) each used roughly 37 to 40 tool calls, and **most facts come from search-result summaries or from fetches summarized by a small model, not from reading primary pages or PDFs in full**. The primary pages the researchers say they read directly were few: the LSC 2022 press release, a Stanford CLP piece, the ABA Journal Utah article, the LawNext piece on LSC's technology report and the Upsolve Supreme Court docket (legal); the IRS e-file provider page (money); the KFF 2024 denials brief, the KFF Health News presumptive-eligibility explainer and the KFF SHIP page (health); and ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer, Code for America's report, Nava's case study and Technical.ly (precedents). This report keeps the researchers' flags: a figure marked "(secondary)" came from a vendor, blog, aggregator or low-authority page, and a figure marked "(pre-2022)" is older than 2022. Claims the researchers could not tie to a source URL are omitted. Several widely repeated numbers are explicitly unreliable and not used, such as the "80% of medical bills contain errors" claim, for which the researchers found no traceable primary study. Costs from commercial sites (lawyer fees, DIY-document prices, advocate rates) are indicative, not measured. Where the notes disagree, this report says so rather than choosing.

## Medical paperwork combines the largest documented uptake gaps with the lowest legal friction

**Hospital charity care is the strongest single candidate.** Dollar For, the nonprofit that works this problem, says only **29% of eligible patients get hospital aid**, a figure relayed by a secondary roundup rather than read on its own page ([Houston Public Media](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/health-science/healthcare/2026/10/28/565447/texas-nonprofit-hospitals-must-offer-charity-care-but-many-patients-never-realize-they-can-apply/)). In Oregon, where hospitals must screen patients, **about 80% of those helped in 2025 never applied** ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/medical-debt-hospital-bills-presumptive-eligibility-explainer-charity-care/)); that article also cites an analysis finding hospitals billed patients at least **$2 billion** in a year that they likely did not owe. The exclusion is procedural: federal rules (IRC 501(r)) cover only nonprofit hospitals, require only "reasonable efforts" to screen, and give patients **240 days** from the first post-discharge bill to apply, with a complete application pausing collection (secondary on the 240-day detail). Only six states (CA, DE, IL, MD, NC, OR) mandate presumptive eligibility, and Oregon's legislature weakened its 2023 law in 2026 by raising the screening threshold from $500 to $1,500 per visit ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/medical-debt-hospital-bills-presumptive-eligibility-explainer-charity-care/)). The cost of the status quo is mostly the debt itself: 72% of adults with health care debt say it came from a one-time or short-term expense.

The precedent is real but small. Dollar For built a database of charity-care criteria for more than 2,000 hospitals, maps a patient's answers onto each hospital's form and follows up with the hospital; it helped erase over **$55 million** of bills in 2025, up from about $32 million in 2024, with a staff that grew from one person to about 15 ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/podcast/an-arm-and-a-leg-podcast-charity-care-nonprofit-dollar-for-medical-bills/)). It is fragile: two donors left in early 2025, taking about $500,000, almost a third of its budget, and a planned AI document-checking tool (about $250,000) remains unbuilt. Its approval rates also disagree across sources (57% of applicants in its 2024 report; 42% of fully tracked applications in June 2025), and the notes do not reconcile them ([Dollar For](https://dollarfor.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Dollar_For_Path.pdf); [NY Focus](https://nysfocus.com/2025/06/12/new-york-charity-care)). The opening is therefore scale and upkeep, not a new category: per-hospital policy lookup, an application pre-filled from the hospital's own form, and a deadline tracker. The regulatory burden is light because hospital policies are published documents and the user signs and submits. HIPAA does not reach an app that receives data at the patient's direction and is not acting for a covered entity, but the FTC and state consumer-health privacy laws do ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/access-right-health-apps-apis/index.html)). The real cost is maintaining thousands of hospital policies; a team without Dollar For's database should either partner with it or start in the presumptive-eligibility states, where the rules are most standardized.

**Denial appeals and record requests are second.** KFF found that ACA marketplace insurers denied **19% of in-network claims in 2024 (about 85 million of 451 million)** while **under 1% of denials were appealed** (at least 262,983), and 66% of appealed denials were upheld by the insurer ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/patient-consumer-protections/claims-denials-and-appeals-in-aca-marketplace-plans-in-2024/)). Where people do appeal, results are better than the status quo implies: 2025 prior-authorization denials were overturned on appeal 67% of the time in Medicare Advantage, 47% in Medicaid managed care and 43% in the federal marketplace ([KFF via Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/insurers-denied-between-12-18-prior-auth-requests-2025-kff)). A secondary summary puts the Medicare Advantage overturn rate at 80.7% against an 11.5% appeal rate, a different year and measure that the notes do not reconcile ([MedCity News](https://medcitynews.com/2026/08/kff-insurers-denied-12-18-of-prior-authorization-requests-in-2025/)). On the billing side, 43% of adults and 53% of adults with debt reported receiving a bill they believed was wrong, and 51% of the indebted group could not fix it or did not try ([ConsumerAffairs summary of KFF 2022](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/more-than-half-of-americans-have-owed-medical-debt-in-last-five-years-study-finds-071922.html)); the Commonwealth Fund found fewer than half of those with unexpected bills challenged them, and 38% of disputers got a reduction ([Commonwealth Fund](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/issue-briefs/2024/aug/unforeseen-health-care-bills-coverage-denials-by-insurers)). Because these are suspected rather than confirmed errors, the dollar value per case is unknown. The price to beat is the paid advocate at **25-35% of savings** (vendor pages, secondary); free options exist (Patient Advocate Foundation, Dollar For) but none was found for letter generation at scale. The legal hook is that an ERISA plan may not bar an authorized representative from pursuing a claim or appeal ([DOL Information Letter](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/information-letters/02-27-2019)), though the representative may lack standing to sue. Employer-plan denial and appeal rates, the largest group, were not found, and the researchers did not source state UPL limits for billing advocates, so that gap should be closed before launch. A companion record-request tool (30-day HIPAA deadline, escalation to OCR) is cheap and low-liability but rests on 2019 compliance evidence (pre-2022): a Ciitizen audit found 51% of 51 providers not fully compliant ([Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/most-providers-not-fully-compliant-with-hipaa-access-requirements-research/560954/)).

The weaker medical candidates share one flaw: thin or missing evidence. The No Surprises Act patient dispute process is open only to uninsured or self-pay patients billed at least $400 over a good faith estimate, for a $25 fee ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/gfe-and-ppdr-requirements-slides.pdf)), but **no filing counts were found**, only a 2021 projection of 26,000 cases a year. Prescription comparison is already served by GoodRx and Cost Plus Drugs. Vital-records ordering is a pass-through that requires identity verification. Disability backlogs (about 362,000 pending hearings, 275-day average wait, all from secondary sites) are mostly not addressable by software. Medicare plan choice is the exception worth tracking: SHIP counselors served about 4.3 million people in 2022 on about **$70 million in 2025 funding, roughly $1 per beneficiary**, nearly half of them volunteers, and 65% of beneficiaries did not compare coverage in a recent open enrollment ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicare/the-role-of-ships-in-helping-people-with-medicare-navigate-their-coverage/)). The researchers did not research CMS marketing or insurance-broker licensing rules for an unpaid recommendation tool, which could be the barrier, so the idea stays on the second tier.

## Eviction and debt courts hold the biggest need behind the tightest legal gate

The civil justice gap is the largest unmet need in the notes. LSC's 2022 study found **92% of substantial civil legal problems of low-income Americans got no or insufficient help**, and LSC-funded groups turned away about half of eligible requests, a rate unchanged since 2005 ([LSC](https://www.lsc.gov/press-release/low-income-americans-face-immense-justice-gap-according-new-legal-services-corporation-report)). No newer edition was found. In eviction, tenants are represented about **4%** of the time against 83-84% for landlords ([Pro Bono Institute](https://www.probonoinst.org/2025/07/21/sustaining-tenants-right-to-counsel/)), and the Eviction Tracking System counted **1.23 million filings in 2025** across only 38 city areas and 10 states ([Eviction Lab](https://evictionlab.org/ets-report-2025/)); nationally, 30-50% of tenants lose by default (secondary). In debt collection, Pew's 2020 report is cited for default judgments in more than 70% of suits and about 90% of defendants unrepresented (pre-2022, via secondary citations; the PDF could not be opened), and a Utah study found 53% of defendants with a lawyer won against 19% of self-represented ones ([Pew](https://www.pew.org/-/media/assets/2020/06/debt-collectors-to-consumers.pdf)). The needed product is narrow: a correct, on-time answer. The researchers found no study measuring an online eviction-answer tool's effect on default rates, so the benefit is plausible but unproven.

Free tools exist but are fragmented. LawHelp Interactive reported more than 1 million free sessions and 825,000 document packages in 2022 ([Pro Bono Net](https://www.probono.net/programs/lhi/)); Suffolk's Document Assembly Line (open-source Docassemble) powers Massachusetts Court Forms Online, where **300+ people completed an eviction-record sealing petition in the first week** after the May 2025 law ([State Justice Institute](https://www.sji.gov/national-document-assembly-hosting-and-e-filing-initiative/)). They fall short on state-by-state content, upkeep and reach: Stanford's toolkit says assembly needs a named maintainer, forms vary by state, and a docassemble vendor suggests budgeting 15-20% of annual build cost for maintenance (commercial source). Court gatekeeping can kill a tool outright: a New York court blocked JustFix's e-filing tool in June 2021 after about 3,000 tenants had used it ([Georgetown Law Tech Review](https://georgetownlawtechreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/4.1-Clement_Housing_Formatted.pdf)). The safe lane is legal information plus form assembly using the court's own forms, ideally with court-approved status as Court Forms Online has in Massachusetts. LSC itself recommends funding self-help tools (document assembly, chatbots and triage), and its Technology Initiative Grants have funded 923 grants totaling over $92 million since 2000, though a grant takes 10-12 months to start ([LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/02/lsc-issues-blueprint-for-narrowing-the-justice-gap-through-technology-innovation-in-civil-legal-services.html)).

**The status of *Upsolve v. James* is unverified, and the two research notes disagree.** The notes agree that the Second Circuit vacated, on September 9, 2025, the preliminary injunction that would have let trained non-lawyer "Justice Advocates" advise New York debt-collection defendants, which makes this the case that bears on a debt-answer tool. They differ on what followed. The legal note reports that the Second Circuit ruled against Upsolve, that a cert petition (No. 25-948) was filed February 6, 2026, and that the **Supreme Court denied certiorari on March 30, 2026**, based on the docket the researcher read ([Supreme Court docket](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=%2Fdocket%2Fdocketfiles%2Fhtml%2Fpublic%2F25-948.html); [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10668452/upsolve-inc-v-james/)). The precedents note instead reports that the Second Circuit vacated the injunction and that, **on remand, the district court (Judge Kaplan) ruled against Upsolve in March 2026**, with the cert petition "apparently pending" and its status unconfirmed ([Volokh Conspiracy](https://reason.com/volokh/2026/03/08/court-upholds-new-yorks-limits-on-unauthorized-practice-of-law/)). That note also flags that one of its links is dated 2023, so the opinion it points to needs checking, and that the September 2025 date came from a search summary. This report does not pick between them. What both versions support is the same planning assumption: no near-term constitutional route for non-lawyer advice is established, so a debt-answer product should supply information and court forms and route anything resembling advice to a lawyer or legal aid partner. Before relying on the case's status, someone should pull the Second Circuit opinion and the docket directly.

The alternatives to the nonlawyer route are narrowing. Utah's sandbox fell from 39 entrants in 2022 to 11 as of April 2025 and closed to new applications with a reported 2027 sunset (not verified on the official page); Arizona's 136 approved alternative business structures are concentrated in personal injury; and Stanford found no high-innovation software-without-lawyer entities and only four free low-income entities ([Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/2025/06/02/regulatory-innovation-at-the-crossroads-five-years-of-data-on-entity-regulation-reform-in-arizona-and-utah/); [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/nearly-30-legal-entities-may-leave-utahs-regulatory-sandbox-after-state-tightens-rules)). Record clearing is the legal task where an at-cost model is proven. Rasa Legal charges a flat **$250** plus government fees (the eligibility check is free to $15) for AI-drafted, lawyer-reviewed petitions, against $1,500-$3,000 from a lawyer, and NPR reports 34,000 people cleared ([KSL](https://www.ksl.com/article/50955517/clearing-your-record-just-got-easier-expungement-costs-cut); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/03/nx-s1-5892484/ai-legal-tech-jobs-clean-slate)). Uptake otherwise is low (6.5% of eligible Michigan residents within five years; pre-2022), and automatic clearing (13-14 states with Clean Slate laws, depending on the count) both shrinks the long-run petition market and has data-quality problems. Code for America's Clear My Record was sold to prosecutors and helped reduce or dismiss about **144,000** California marijuana convictions ([Code for America](https://codeforamerica.org/news/record-clearance-at-scale-how-clear-my-record-helped-reduce-or-dismiss-144-000-convictions-in-california/)); the lesson is that the payer is the government that holds the records. Eligibility logic changes with statute every year, which makes record clearing the hardest rules engine in the set. Name changes, wills, small-estate affidavits and divorce have smaller or better-served populations, with divorce adding variable state forms and higher liability. Immigration forms are a poor first build: USCIS fees and rules were in flux in 2026 (a June 2026 proposed rule would remove the reduced N-400 fee) and DACA renewals fell sharply, 53,855 approved in the first half of FY2026 against about 145,000 a year earlier ([KCRA](https://www.kcra.com/article/american-dreams-broken-system-daca-renewal-delays/73619047)).

## Tax, benefits and loan help meet real surges but depend on channels software cannot enter

**Tax filing has the cleanest need and the heaviest fixed barrier.** IRS Direct File is gone: the IRS told states on November 3, 2025 that it would not be available in 2026, with no launch date ([NATP](https://www.natptax.com/news-insights/blog/direct-file-is-gone-what-s-next-for-free-filing-and-tax-pros/)). It had produced **140,803 accepted returns** in its 2024 pilot, and 59% of 751,000 registrants in 2025 did not file (a TIGTA figure relayed by Accounting Today); Treasury later put the cost at at least **$138 per accepted return** against earlier projections of $9-$16, a number driven by a small denominator ([Accounting Today](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/irs-ends-direct-file-program)). What remains is Free File, used by only about **3%** of eligible taxpayers ([CRS IF11808](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11808)), and VITA/TCE, where 479 applicants requested more than $79 million against $53 million awarded ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/irs-2026-tax-advice-grants-11447268)). The unclaimed-credit gap is real: about 19.2% of eligible taxpayers did not claim the EITC for tax year 2022 ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/eitc-central/eitc-participation-rate-by-state)), and the Chicago Fed estimated more than $4 billion to about $11 billion unclaimed for 2019 (pre-2022) ([Chicago Fed](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/profitwise-news-and-views/2022/millions-tax-refunds-unclaimed)). The build is the problem: becoming an e-file provider requires an EFIN, fingerprinting for principals, a suitability check the IRS says can take up to 45 days, and MeF software testing ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/e-file-providers/become-an-authorized-e-file-provider)); unconsented disclosure of return data is a misdemeanor under IRC 7216, and the FTC Safeguards Rule requires a written security program at any size (secondary summaries). The archived Direct File code and Fact Graph are public domain and reduce the tax-logic cost, but the repo warns of unpatched vulnerabilities and the notes do not show it covers post-2025 law changes ([GitHub](https://github.com/IRS-Public/direct-file)). Free File's 3% take-up suggests free software alone does not change behavior, so a team would need prepare-and-print or a partner channel, not a new transmitter.

**Benefits paperwork has a time-boxed job but no submission channel.** Take-up is high for SNAP (88% of eligible individuals in FY2022) and low for WIC (about 54-56%) ([USDA FNS](https://www.fns.usda.gov/newsroom/subscriber-updates/snap/fy22trends); [USDA WIC](https://www.fna.usda.gov/research/wic/eer/2023)). The sharper problem is paperwork churn: the Medicaid unwinding disenrolled at least 25.2 million people, **69% procedurally** ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/as-medicaid-unwinding-concludes-in-most-states-kff-finds-25-million-lost-medicaid-coverage-but-enrollment-is-10-million-higher-than-pre-pandemic-levels/)). New Medicaid work-reporting rules for expansion adults take effect **January 1, 2027 in 44 states**, with CMS's interim final rule effective July 31, 2026; a federal court vacated part of the rule's medical-frailty language on October 29, 2026, but the January date stands ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/11/medicaid-work-requirements-remain-on-track-despite-partial-vacatur)). SNAP's work-requirement age range already expanded to 18-64 on November 1, 2025 (secondary). A 2023 CBO report found over 92% of adult Medicaid enrollees already worked, studied or were exempt, so expected losses are verification failures, not unemployment. The vendors building the state systems serve agencies, so an individual-facing "document and report my hours" tool is an inferred gap rather than a documented one. The channel problem is decisive: the only standardized API found (Patient Access API) retrieves data and does not submit applications ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/03/04/2019-02200/medicare-and-medicaid-programs-patient-protection-and-affordable-care-act-interoperability-and)), and no source covers state portal terms of service or scraping law, so the tractable design is screening, document packaging and renewal reminders rather than portal submission. Open-source screeners already exist: MyFriendBen (Colorado, built on PolicyEngine's API) had a median completion time of 6.6 minutes and about two-thirds of users learned of at least one new benefit ([Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/myfriendben-benefits-screener)).

**Student loans and credit disputes are secondary.** SAVE ended by court order on March 10, 2026, the Repayment Assistance Plan launched July 1, 2026, and about 7.2 million borrowers were still on SAVE with a 90-day clock (secondary sources as of early 2026; garnishment status unverified) ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/adamminsky/2026/03/16/ominous-student-loan-backlogs-persist-as-7-million-borrowers-are-soon-forced-to-change-plans/)). It is the most time-sensitive consumer-paperwork surge of late 2026, but federal portals and servicers are the only submission channels and the rules are volatile. Credit-report disputes are pure document generation with a free statutory right: the CFPB received about 5.8 million credit-reporting complaints in 2025, though a secondary source says credit-repair firms drove much of that volume and only 0.02% ended in monetary relief ([CFPB](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_2025-cr-annual-report_2026-03.pdf)). The feasibility is high and the need is modest, so it is a module, not a mission. The unbanked share is shrinking (4.2% of households in 2023, FDIC) and bank-access tools matter less than paperwork around collections, disputes and loan plans ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2024/fdic-survey-finds-96-percent-us-households-were-banked-2023)).

## One ranking, built by scoring need and feasibility on a single scale

The four researchers produced provisional rankings on different yardsticks. The legal note ranked by volume times low representation times deadline pain, which put eviction and debt answers first and treated feasibility as a caveat. The health note ranked by people affected times uptake gap times regulatory friction, which is already a need-times-feasibility score and put charity care first. The money note did not rank and offered only inferences, singling out EITC and child-credit help and a work-reporting tool as the strongest gaps. The precedents note added a filter: choose a single task with a clear goal, public or user-supplied data and a payer who sees the savings. To reconcile them, this report scores each candidate for need (1-5: people excluded, documented gap, cost to the person today) and feasibility (1-5: UPL and licensing exposure, whether the tool must hold sensitive data, whether a submission channel exists, upkeep burden, and whether a precedent proves the model), multiplies, and breaks ties by need. The scores are this report's judgment from the notes, not measured quantities.

| Rank | Service | Need | Feasibility | Product | Deciding factor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hospital charity-care application and 240-day tracker | 4 | 4 | 16 | 29% uptake; published policies; user signs; Dollar For proves demand but not scale |
| 2 | Denial appeal letters with authorized-representative form, plus record and itemized-bill requests | 4 | 3 | 12 | Under 1% appealed; ERISA hook; employer-plan data and state UPL limits unsourced |
| 3 | Eviction answer (one uniform-form state, court partner) | 5 | 2 | 10 | 4% represented; per-state procedure; JustFix block; court approval is the shield |
| 4 | Medicaid/SNAP work-reporting documentation | 4 | 2 | 8 | Jan 1, 2027 deadline; need inferred; no submission API |
| 5 | Record-clearing eligibility and petition | 4 | 2 | 8 | Rasa proves $250 at cost; hardest rules engine; automation erodes market |
| 6 | Medicare plan choice from a user's drug list | 4 | 2 | 8 | SHIP at $1 per beneficiary; CMS marketing and broker rules unresearched |
| 7 | Tax: EITC/CTC claim help via prepare-and-print or partner | 3 | 2 | 6 | Fixed EFIN and 7216 costs; Free File shows 3% take-up |
| 8 | Student-loan RAP/IDR/PSLF helper | 3 | 2 | 6 | Real surge; federal portals only; volatile rules |
| 9 | Good faith estimate checker and PPDR starter | 2 | 3 | 6 | No filing counts; best built as a module of ranks 1-2 |
| 10 | Credit-dispute letters | 2 | 3 | 6 | Free statutory right and pure document generation, but low need and credit-repair noise |
| 11 | Name change, wills, small estates | 2 | 3 | 6 | Court packets and cheap forms already exist |
| 12 | Debt-collection answer | 5 | 1 | 5 | Largest need, and the case where Upsolve v. James bites |

The reasoning behind the main moves is as follows. Eviction and debt answers fall from the legal note's first and second places because that note's own barriers section concludes that UPL law and court e-filing are the binding constraint, and the *Upsolve v. James* uncertainty keeps debt answers at feasibility 1 under either version of the case. Tax falls from the money note's "strongest unmet need" because the fixed regulatory cost does not scale down to a small team and the precedent shows free software alone changes little. Charity care rises because it is the only candidate where need, a published-document workflow, a user-signed submission and a working precedent all coincide, and appeals follow because they share the same user-as-actor structure. Not ranked, on the evidence: immigration forms (volatile rules), protective orders (confidentiality and safety risk, forms already free in every state), SSDI (waiting is not software-addressable), vital records and REAL ID (identity-verification pass-through), prescription prices (incumbents), voucher search (the bottleneck is landlord behavior) and caregiving coordination (a large population of 63 million caregivers but no defined unmet task). Ranks 1, 2 and 9 share infrastructure (an authorized-representative form generator, a deadline tracker, a letter builder, a bill and estimate ingest), so the practical first build is one medical-paperwork application whose first module is charity care.

## Funding and operating models that keep a small service alive

The precedents show that **cost per person is an artifact of what is counted**, and a small team's fixed costs divided by few users dominate. Direct File's $138 per return came from a pilot-scale denominator against a $9-$16 projection at 50-state scale; a Benefits Data Trust application cost "a few hundred dollars" (secondary staff accounts) against about $2 for lean online tools ([Technical.ly](https://technical.ly/civic-news/benefits-data-trust-shutdown-trooper-sanders/)). Benefits Data Trust closed in August 2024 with 273 layoffs on a budget over $30 million, two-thirds philanthropy, after a $20 million gift did not close a structural gap, because states paid for one or two benefits while it staffed for ten to fifteen. Software-only tools avoid that cost but concede the higher success rate of human follow-up. Direct File shows that a well-rated public service (90% positive among 11,000 surveyed users) can still be shut for political reasons; open-sourcing was the only lasting part. The durable pattern is a payer other than the individual: Code for America's GetCalFresh helped 6.2 million people claim $12.8 billion in SNAP benefits and was sunset into the state's own BenefitsCal, a planned handoff ([CDSS](https://cdss.ca.gov/inforesources/cdss-programs/calfresh-outreach/getcalfresh-transition)); Clear My Record sells to prosecutors; and Suffolk's lab blends a State Justice Institute grant, court and legal-aid service contracts, and a Hewlett grant, aiming for ongoing court funding ([Suffolk LIT Lab](https://suffolklitlab.org/reintroducing-the-document-assembly-line/)).

For a team of a few developers, the nonprofit scale to calibrate against is modest. Upsolve has run at **$1.8-$2.1 million of expenses** a year since 2021 with revenue swinging between $1.2 million and $3.7 million, funded about 70% by grants and donations and about 30% by program service revenue including small partner referral fees, plus a $4.25 million Gates grant in 2024 ([ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/821736267); [Upsolve](https://upsolve.org/learn/how-upsolve-is-free/)). JustFix spent $912,000 against $814,000 of revenue in 2024 and has run deficits every year since 2020, drawing net assets from $1.55 million to $454,000 ([ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/813080695)). A venture-backed route exists (Propel raised about $68 million and monetizes through coupons, ads, jobs and card interchange), but it means selling the users' attention. The models by revenue source in the notes are 501(c)(3) for grants (Upsolve, Dollar For, JustFix, mRelief, Code for America), a public benefit corporation for government contracts (Nava, with 71% of federal obligations from CMS per an aggregator) and venture for ad-and-offer revenue. The notes found no evidence on fiscal sponsorship or sliding-scale fees for this category. The operating model that follows from the evidence has three layers. Build and iteration is grant-funded; the notes' Technology Initiative Grant math (32 grants totaling $4.2 million, about $130,000 each by this report's arithmetic) shows grants of that size fund a prototype, not a run. The run layer, hosting, upkeep, support and the annual re-audit of forms or policies, needs a payer: a court, a state agency, legal aid or a foundation line item, and Suffolk's LSC-funded DevOps hire shows that even the best-known project is still seeking that money. The hidden layer is cyber and liability insurance (about $4,000-$10,000 a year for a social-services nonprofit holding health information and a $2 million limit, per insurer blogs), security and accessibility reviews, and support; no source totals these for a small service. Concentration is the survivable risk to plan for: Dollar For lost nearly a third of its budget when two donors left.

Design choices cut costs as much as funding does. Do not hold the sensitive data: Code for America's tool runs on bulk records the prosecutor holds, and Dollar For maps answers onto the hospital's form and submits (the notes did not check whether these tools retain data). Avoid products that need government-grade identity proofing: Login.gov charges agencies $3 per active user in a proofing year with a $2,500 monthly minimum, ID.me sent 10-15% of applicants to video review, and experts estimate proofing works for about 80% of people ([GAO-25-107000](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-25-107000.pdf); [Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/digital-government/2022/01/no-easy-fix-id-verification-government-benefits/361120/), 2022). Substantiate every claim: the FTC's DoNotPay order ($193,000) bars "AI lawyer" claims made without testing, and its Intuit order targeted misleading "free" tax advertising ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-finalizes-order-donotpay-prohibits-deceptive-ai-lawyer-claims-imposes-monetary-relief-requires)). Use AI only in the pattern the evidence supports: retrieval over curated, state-specific sources with a refusal path and human review. A Nava trial with 125 caseworkers found about 40% accuracy gains but also that tools need training and support, while Stanford's study found purpose-built legal research tools still erred 17-33% of the time (2024) ([Nava](https://www.navapbc.com/case-studies/evaluating-ai-assistive-chatbot-caseworkers); [Stanford](https://dho.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/Legal_RAG_Hallucinations.pdf)). Reach follows intermediaries: LSC refers overflow to Upsolve, and Dollar For grew through press coverage, not channel partnerships. Two further revenue ideas are this report's own hypotheses, not findings: a hospital-facing offer (nearly 90% of tax-exempt hospitals used presumptive eligibility as of 2022, so automating the screening step could be sold to the institutions that must do it) and open-sourcing the course content so a state or court can run it after funding ends. The notes do not test either.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which service has the most need" to "which service can a few developers run without a lawyer, a license, an IRS credential or a state's cooperation." Judged that way, the highest-need legal and tax problems are the hardest and the medical-paperwork problems are the easiest, and the two groups are almost uncorrelated, which is why a need-only ranking and a need-times-feasibility ranking give nearly opposite orders. The precedents add a second filter that the need data cannot supply: the survivors have a payer who pays for running costs or a design that keeps running costs near zero, and the failures are about unit economics and political dependence, not technology.

Three things would most change this ranking and should be verified first. The *Upsolve v. James* docket and opinions must be pulled directly, since it decides whether debt-collection answers move from last to a feasible item. Employer-plan denial and appeal rates, state UPL limits on billing advocates and CMS marketing rules for Medicare tools are all unresearched and could move ranks 2 and 6. And Dollar For's willingness to partner or share its hospital database determines whether the top-ranked service is a build or a collaboration; the notes show it is a 15-person organization that lost a third of its budget in a year, which makes it as likely to welcome a partner as to treat one as a competitor.
